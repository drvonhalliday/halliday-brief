From the Desk

The legislative interim is when the structural conversations happen. The Emergency Response Services Committee's February briefing — three months ago and still untouched in the broader policy discourse — produced the first statewide quantification of what ND ambulance providers have been saying operationally for years: revenue per transport averages $1,100, expenses average $1,600, and the gap is being closed by tax subsidies, fundraisers, and service reductions. That is the lead story this week, because the committee is now drafting legislation for the 2027 session and CAH boards have an operational reason to track it: ambulance fiscal distress is an upstream variable that shapes patient acuity, transfer feasibility, and inpatient throughput.

At the federal level, CMS imposed two companion nationwide enrollment moratoria on hospices and home health agencies effective May 13 — a program-integrity action with narrow but real implications for ND CAHs considering structural changes to home health or hospice service lines through year-end. HRSA's Rural Communities Opioid Response Program reopened in two tracks with deadlines in the next two weeks; for ND organizations with existing partnership infrastructure, both windows are worth understanding. Four watch items round out the issue.

State Policy
North Dakota · Emergency Medical Services · Fiscal Crisis

$500 Lost Per Transport: PWW Survey Documents ND Ambulance Fiscal Collapse

A survey of 36 North Dakota ambulance providers presented to the Legislature's interim Emergency Response Services Committee in February documented an average loss of approximately $500 per patient transport in 2025, with statewide unpaid claims estimated at $5.8 million across more than 100 providers. The committee, chaired by Rep. Jim Grueneich (R-Ellendale), is drafting legislation for the 2027 session. For CAH administrators, the ambulance fiscal trajectory is now an upstream variable that shapes patient acuity on arrival, transfer feasibility, and inpatient throughput.

The PWW Advisory Group survey was authorized by House Bill 1322, passed during the 2025 legislative session, and presented to the Emergency Response Services Committee on February 25, 2026.1 The survey covered 36 ND ambulance providers and found that the average revenue generated per patient transport in 2025 was approximately $1,100, against average expenses of approximately $1,600 — a loss of roughly $500 per call. Matt Zavadsky, an EMS and mobile health care consultant with PWW, told the committee that providers "are losing money every time they respond to a call" and that the structural shortfall could not be closed by billing reform alone, because the revenue generated by transport-based reimbursement does not fund the cost of readiness — 24/7 staffing, equipment, and supplies that must be maintained regardless of call volume.1 The total estimated unpaid claims across more than 100 ND providers in 2025 was approximately $5.8 million.1

Rep. Todd Porter (R-Mandan), who owns Metro-Area Ambulance Service in the Bismarck-Mandan area, provided the structural framing. Medicare patients make up roughly 60 percent of his service's call volume, and Medicare's reimbursement rates fall below the cost of providing the service. "If we're being underpaid for 60 percent of our call volume, then we have to make it up some place," Porter said. Some providers can offset the gap with tax-supported revenue through ambulance districts or municipal subsidies. Others rely on contracted work with nursing homes, hospitals, and funeral homes to maintain solvency. Porter characterized the underlying problem as federal: "This is a federal government problem. This is a CMS problem that we've known about for years."1

The fiscal trap that complicates state-level response is the interaction between newly formed ambulance districts and the property tax mill levy caps under HB 1168 and HB 1176. Districts that form to provide stable revenue for 24/7 EMS coverage may find their initial levy authority constrained by the 3 percent year-over-year cap, leaving the gap between authorized revenue and actual cost of service unaddressed.2

The Emergency Response Services Committee, formed by the 2025 legislative session and chaired by Rep. Grueneich, has committed to a multi-meeting interim process culminating in draft legislation for the 2027 session.3 The committee's mandate includes the volunteer responder recruitment study under HB 1311, the delinquent billing reimbursement grant feasibility study under HB 1322, and broader work on EMS workforce, district formation, and reimbursement structure.3

Federal action remains the larger structural lever. The Medicare ground ambulance add-on payments — 2 percent for urban, 3 percent for rural, and 22.6 percent for super-rural transports — have been extended through December 31, 2027.2 The CARE Act (S. 3145, Sens. Collins and Welch) and the EMS ROCS Act (S. 3730 / H.R. 7277, Sens. Welch and Sanders, Rep. Balint), both reintroduced in Q4 2025 and Q1 2026, would establish reimbursement pathways for treatment-in-place — addressing the structural mismatch between current "no transport, no pay" Medicare rules and the clinical reality that many EMS calls are appropriately resolved without ED transport.4 Neither has advanced to floor consideration.

Von Halliday Analysis

For CAH administrators, ambulance fiscal distress is not a peripheral concern. The clinical and operational connections are direct: a district that cannot sustain 24/7 coverage means longer dispatch-to-scene times for patients in your catchment, higher acuity on arrival at your ED, and reduced feasibility of routine interfacility transfers to tertiary facilities. The PWW survey makes it possible for the first time to quantify what CAH administrators have been observing operationally.

Boards in service areas where the local ambulance provider is fiscally distressed should consider whether existing contractual or partnership arrangements — interfacility transfer contracts, community paramedicine pilots reimbursed under ND Medicaid's expanded coverage, or service-line cost-sharing — can be structured to provide partial financial stability without creating CAH liability for EMS operating losses. The 2027 legislative session is when the structural response will be debated. The interim is when CAH-level operational adaptations need to be in place.

Federal Policy
CMS · Program Integrity · Hospice & Home Health Enrollment

CMS Imposes Nationwide Six-Month Moratorium on New Hospice and Home Health Enrollment

Effective May 13, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services has blocked new Medicare enrollment of hospices and home health agencies nationwide for six months, citing program integrity concerns. Existing enrolled providers are unaffected. The action's operational consequences fall on a narrow but consequential group: organizations planning acquisitions, ownership transitions, or new service line conversions in the next six months. For ND CAHs that operate or are affiliated with home health and hospice service lines, the moratorium is worth understanding even if it doesn't immediately apply.

CMS published two companion Federal Register notices on May 13, 2026 — Document No. 2026-09717 for home health agencies and Document No. 2026-09718 for hospices — announcing a six-month nationwide moratorium on new Medicare enrollment.5 The action invokes the agency's authority under section 1866(j)(7) of the Social Security Act and 42 CFR § 424.570, which permits temporary enrollment moratoria where CMS identifies "significant potential for fraud, waste, or abuse." The moratoria apply to all states, territories, and the District of Columbia and may be extended in six-month increments.5

The moratoria do not affect existing enrolled providers. Hospices and home health agencies currently delivering Medicare services continue to operate and bill normally. Applications received by Medicare Administrative Contractors before May 13 are also exempt under the grandfathering provision at 42 CFR § 424.570(a)(1)(iv); organizations with pending applications submitted before the effective date should retain documentation of the submission date.6

The operational complication lies in the interaction between the moratorium and the 36-month change-in-majority-ownership rule at 42 CFR § 424.550(b). Under that rule, a hospice or HHA undergoing a change in majority ownership within 36 months of its initial enrollment or its most recent CIMO must enroll as a new provider and obtain a new state survey or accreditation.6 Because the moratorium blocks new enrollments, it also blocks any transaction that would trigger the 36-month CIMO rule. Asset acquisitions, ownership transitions involving entities within their first 36 months of operation, and structural reorganizations that require a new CMS Certification Number are effectively paused through approximately November 13, 2026.

CMS framed the action as a continuation of the program integrity package that began with the February 27 nationwide moratorium on Durable Medical Equipment, Prosthetics, Orthotics, and Supplies enrollment.7 The agency cited enrollment growth in specific geographies — including a more than 40 percent increase in Los Angeles County HHA enrollments between 2019 and 2023 and clusters of multiple HHAs operating from single addresses in states including Ohio — as evidence of nationwide rather than localized fraud risk.6 The American Hospital Association's response, issued through senior vice president Ashley Thompson, supported the program integrity objective but flagged a specific rural concern: that "many rural and underserved areas of the country" depend on home health and hospice providers to ensure timely and appropriate post-discharge care.7

The state-level wrinkle worth tracking: CMS has indicated that each state will decide whether to apply parallel moratoria to its Medicaid and CHIP programs.5 ND HHS has not announced a position. The Brief will track this as the moratorium period progresses.

Von Halliday Analysis

The direct operational impact on ND CAHs is narrow but real. CAHs that currently operate home health or hospice service lines under their existing Medicare provider agreements are not affected — service delivery, billing, and recertification all continue normally. The concern is for boards considering structural changes: consolidating an independent hospice under hospital ownership, transitioning a county-operated home health agency to CAH operation, or any acquisition that would create a new Medicare provider for enrollment purposes. Those transactions are blocked through November 13 at the earliest.

CAH boards with pending or contemplated home health or hospice transactions should consult counsel before the next six-month period begins to evaluate whether the structure can be modified to avoid the 36-month CIMO trigger, or whether the transaction simply needs to wait.

HRSA · Federal Office of Rural Health Policy · SUD/OUD Funding

Two Federal Rural SUD Funding Windows Close in the Next Two Weeks

HRSA's Rural Communities Opioid Response Program is open in two tracks with deadlines May 29 and June 1. Planning awards run $100K per year over two years; Impact awards run $750K per year over four. Both require multi-sector consortia and rural service-area eligibility.

HRSA's Federal Office of Rural Health Policy released two RCORP notices of funding opportunity in April. The Planning track (HRSA-26-046) makes $4 million available across 40 awards of up to $100,000 per year over a two-year project period. Eligible activities include needs assessments, partnership development, and the design of sustainable SUD/OUD service infrastructure; direct service delivery is not allowable. Applications close May 29. The Impact track (HRSA-26-037) makes $60 million available across 80 awards of up to $750,000 per year over a four-year project period, funding evidence-based prevention, treatment, recovery, and workforce activities. Applications close June 1.8

Both tracks require applicants to organize as multi-sector consortia. For ND organizations, the natural consortium structure draws on existing working relationships: a CAH or FQHC as the lead applicant with partners from tribal health programs, behavioral health agencies, public health units, county human service zones, and recovery-community organizations. RCORP applications are scored substantially on partnership strength, integration with primary care and behavioral health, and credibility of the sustainability plan beyond the period of performance.

The runway concern is registration. Both grants.gov and SAM.gov registration typically takes several weeks. HRSA does not grant deadline extensions for incomplete registrations. Organizations not currently registered are unlikely to meet either deadline.

Von Halliday Analysis

The Planning track is the structurally appropriate entry for ND organizations that have been considering an SUD expansion but have not yet built the consortium infrastructure to support it. The Impact track assumes existing partnerships, data infrastructure, and a credible exit plan. Boards considering Impact should ask whether the consortium can absorb a four-year award and sustain services after federal funding ends.

What to Watch
  • 1
    CMS Medicaid work requirements IFR — statutory deadline June 1. The interim final rule implementing the OBBBA community engagement requirements has been at OMB review since April 1. The statutory deadline is two weeks out. CMS's prior record on related rulemaking timelines suggests the deadline may slip.9 North Dakota Medicaid has not announced an implementation date. The IFR's treatment of medical frailty exemptions, caregiver definitions, ex parte renewal requirements, and the good-faith-effort extension pathway will shape what CAH and FQHC revenue cycle planning needs to look like through the back half of 2026 and into the January 1, 2027 implementation date.
  • 2
    Funding deadlines — federal and state. The May 22 RHTP cluster closes Friday: the $2.6M Community-Based Walking Programs grant, $700K Zero-Hour PE Initiative, $300K Community Gardens Project, and the $2.31M Rightsizing Health Care Delivery Systems technical assistance grant for rural FQHCs and CAHs.10 The Behavioral Health Promotion Community Grants ($1.6M / 10 awards) and Ensuring Safety Net Service Delivery grant close May 29. HRSA's new Rural Hospital Provider Assistance Program — $25M formula grant authorized by the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 — closes July 1, with the NOFO expected in May. HRSA's preliminary eligibility analysis identified 167 hospitals across 13 states meeting the program's bed and wage-index criteria. None are in North Dakota.11 The wage index threshold (less than 0.90) structurally excludes ND hospitals from this program, a point worth flagging for delegation offices engaged on rural hospital payment formulas.
  • 3
    FY 2027 IPPS proposed rule comment period closes June 9. CMS-1849-P (April 10, 2026) includes the proposed 2.4 percent operating payment rate increase, the discontinuation of the low-wage index hospital policy with a narrow transitional exception, a 3.3 percent reduction in DSH uncompensated care payments, expanded mandatory Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement (CJR-X) model, and the GME 90 percent rule.12 The rule also notes that, absent legislative action, the Medicare-Dependent Hospital program and the temporary low-volume hospital payment adjustment will expire December 31, 2026 — a national rural payment cliff that ND's predominantly Critical Access Hospital landscape is structurally insulated from but worth understanding as advocacy context.
  • 4
    Dakota Conference on Rural Health — June 3–4, Grand Forks. The region's longest-running gathering of rural health practitioners, hospital leaders, public health officials, tribal health representatives, and state policymakers. Hosted by the UND Center for Rural Health at the Alerus Center.13 For ND CAH boards, two days of plenaries, breakouts, and unstructured time with peer organizations, state agency leadership, and the policy community.
